# Writing the caption: reasoning and patterns

Read this when the post doesn't fit the usual shape, or when the person questions a choice. The checker can count things, but it can't tell whether a caption is worth reading.

## Why the job decides everything

People see a caption in two very different moments.

**Under a photo or carousel**, the viewer has stopped on an image and their eyes drop to the text. They read two lines at most, then either tap "more", swipe, or move on. Those two lines are the hook. A carousel's first slide and first caption line work as a pair: the slide shows, and the line says why it matters. Don't make them say the same thing.

**Under a Reel**, the viewer is already watching, and the caption is one faint line over the video. Few people read it while the video plays. It gets read afterwards, by people who liked the video and want more. So a Reel caption:

- adds what the video only implied: the number, the name of the tool, the steps in order, the source
- states the takeaway in words someone could search for
- makes the one ask

Repeating the Reel's first spoken line is wasted space, because the viewer just heard it.

## The first line

The line has to make sense on its own and fit before the cut. Things that work:

- **A result with a number:** "I cut my grocery bill by $212 a month."
- **A specific mistake:** "I watered my fiddle-leaf fig every Sunday. That's what killed it."
- **A named thing and a claim about it:** "Lisbon's cheapest flat white is in a hardware store."
- **For a Reel, the thing the video didn't say:** "The app is called Streaks, and the setting is under Reminders."

Things that waste it:

- an announcement ("Here's what I learned…", "So, this happened")
- a question with an obvious answer
- a run of emoji
- the account's tagline
- a hashtag

The checker flags some of these patterns. It can't spot a line that is grammatically fine and still says nothing, so read the first line on its own and ask: would a stranger know what this post is about?

## Search terms

Instagram matches a search against the words in captions (see `facts.md`). Write the search terms the way people type them, once each, inside real sentences. "Meal prep for one" belongs in a sentence like "Meal prep for one is mostly about portions." A hashtag of the same words doesn't count for the check: a tag is not a sentence, and the habit leads to tag-only captions.

Two or three terms is plenty. More than that turns the caption into a list of keywords.

## One ask

Several asks compete with each other, and a reader who has to choose often does nothing. This is the skill's own rule, not something Instagram measures. Choose by what the post is:

| The post | Ask that fits |
|---|---|
| teaches steps someone will use later | save it |
| someone will recognise a friend in it | send it to that person |
| offers a resource (a template, a list) | comment a keyword to receive it, or "link in bio" |
| opens a question people have opinions on | comment their answer, with the question made specific |
| one of a series | follow for the next part, naming what the next part is |

Write the ask as a plain instruction. "Comment SHEET and I'll send it" beats "Let me know in the comments if you'd like the sheet!".

## Hashtags

Hashtags are optional. They help a post get found, not seen by more people. If you use them, pick specific tags that someone interested in this exact topic would follow or search, and put them at the end. Stay under the limit. Don't repeat the search terms as tags just to have both.

## Emoji

An emoji can mark a list item or stand in for a word. A row of them adds nothing, and at the start of a photo caption they use up preview space that words should fill. The checker's density limit is a house rule. If the account's style uses more, raise `--emoji-per-100-words`.

## Length

There's no right length. A Reel caption is often three to six short lines, while a carousel that tells a story can run long, because the people who tapped "more" asked for it. Cut anything that doesn't help someone who has already decided to read.

## Formatting that survives pasting

Use blank lines between paragraphs. Leave out markdown (`**bold**` shows up as asterisks) and fancy Unicode "bold" letters (search and screen readers can't read them). Put numbers and names as plain text.
